Guide · Literacy · Any subject

Every lesson is a literacy lesson.

Five habits that help students read, talk and write like experts in your subject, plus a printable vocabulary card.

Why it matters. Every subject has its own way of reading, writing and talking. The EEF's guidance for secondary schools calls this disciplinary literacy, and its first recommendation is that every teacher, not only the English department, teaches it. When students can't read the text or write the answer, they can't show what they know.

The 5 habits

  1. 1

    Choose 3 words worth teaching

    Not every new word needs teaching. Skip the everyday words students already know, and pick a few of the other two kinds each lesson:

    Everyday wordshappy, run, table. Students already know these.
    Academic wordsevaluate, justify, significant. They appear in every subject and quietly decide exam marks.
    Subject wordsphotosynthesis, metaphor, denominator. They need explicit teaching.

    Try: say the word, have the class say it back, give a student-friendly meaning, then two examples from your subject.

  2. 2

    Check readability before you hand it out

    Run the text through a readability check first (Microsoft Word has one built in, the Flesch reading ease score). A low score tells you whether the challenge is your subject or the sentences.

    Fix: split long sentences, pre-teach two key words, or read the first paragraph aloud together.

  3. 3

    Read it like an expert

    Think aloud as you read a text in your subject: what you notice first, what you skip, what you check twice. A scientist reads a graph differently from how a historian reads a source. Show them how.

    Say: “Before I read the method, I look at the title and the axes. Watch what I do next.”

  4. 4

    Talk before writing

    Students explain the idea out loud to a partner first, then write it. Speaking it helps them sort out what they think before they put it on paper.

    Model the step up: everyday (“it got hot”) → teacher (“heat was released”) → expert (“the reaction is exothermic”).

  5. 5

    Model the writing, then fade it

    Write one answer live in front of the class and explain each choice. Then write one together. Then let them try alone. Give sentence starters at first, and remove them as students grow in confidence.

    Starter: “This shows that … because …”

Make it stick

Do

  • Plan the 3 words when you plan the lesson
  • Bring old words back in your starters and questions
  • Ask for full answers that use the key word
  • Agree the key words with your department

Avoid

  • Long word lists that nobody uses again
  • Copying definitions from a glossary
  • Handing out a text you haven't read aloud yourself
  • Marking spelling only, and never meaning
